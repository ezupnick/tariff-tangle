# Tariff Crush: Sourced Fact Sheet

Research compiled November 5, 2026, for the Canadian American Business Council holiday game. Every figure below is tied to a source. This is neutral reference material: facts and attributed estimates only.

## How to read this sheet

- Confidence is marked High, Medium, or Low on the figures that matter.
- Contested or fast-moving numbers are flagged. The tariff landscape is changing week to week in late 2026, so anything about current rates needs a re-check right before launch.
- A few authoritative primary sites could not be reached from this research environment (Yale Budget Lab, Peterson Institute, congress.gov/CRS, census.gov, cvma.ca, Agriculture Canada). Those figures come from reliable secondary reporting that quotes them. Before publishing anything CABC-branded, click through to the primary URLs in the source list to lock exact wording. See the pre-launch checklist at the end.

## The one-line honesty rule for this game

There is no single "tariff rate on Canada." It depends entirely on the product and whether it meets USMCA rules of origin. That nuance is not a problem to hide, it is the most accurate and most educational thing the game can teach. Build the mechanic around "it depends on the product," not around one scary number.

## 1. Core framing facts (the relationship)

These are the least contested, most intuitive facts. Lead with them.

- **Canada is the United States' single largest export customer. The US sells more to Canada than to any other country** (about $349B in US exports to Canada in 2024). Canada is the #2 overall two-way goods partner behind Mexico, and was #1 as recently as 2022. Source: US Census Bureau. Confidence: High.
- **Two-way US-Canada trade in goods and services was about $917 billion in 2024**, roughly $2.5 billion every day. 2025 came in lower (~$872-880B) as tariffs bit. Source: trade.gov, USTR, BEA. Confidence: High on scale, Medium on the provisional 2025 figure. Use 2024 as the "normal relationship" baseline.
- **Auto parts cross the border multiple times during manufacturing.** The verified anchor: one real part, a striker plate, crosses the US-Canada border four times on its way into a car (NBC News, 2025, Confidence High). The famous "seven or eight times" is an industry estimate (Alliance of Automobile Manufacturers; Cato; CSIS) with no clean primary study behind it, so label it an estimate, not a fact. Confidence on "7-8": Low. Design note: use "4 crossings" as the truth and "up to 7 or 8" as an illustration of complexity.
- **About 50% of the content of a vehicle built in Canada originates in the United States.** Source: USTR 2024 USMCA Autos Report to Congress. Confidence: High. This is the best "we build things together" stat.
- **Jobs: the recognizable figure is "nearly 9 million US jobs depend on trade with Canada"** (US Chamber of Commerce), but it dates to about 2017-2018. On the Canadian side, current and primary: about 1.7 million Canadian jobs (10.4% of all Canadian jobs) depend on US demand, and 70% of Canada's exports go to the US (Statistics Canada, 2024, Confidence High). If precision matters, pair the Chamber number with the StatCan numbers and cite the year.
- **"Number of states that count Canada as their top export market" has fallen and must carry a year.** Historically cited as 34 to 36 states; 2025 data put it at about 25-26 (Mexico overtook in several). Source: Progressive Policy Institute (36, 2023), Visual Capitalist (25, 2025), Business Data Lab (26, 2025). Confidence: Medium. Safest game phrasing: "Canada is a top-two export market for most US states," or cite one specific number with its year. Do not use the old "35 states" without dating it.

## 2. How the tariffs actually work right now (the mechanic truth)

This section is the engine of the game's accuracy. All of it is fast-moving; re-verify at launch.

- **The USMCA shield is the key mechanism: roughly 85-90% of Canadian goods can cross the border duty-free when they meet USMCA/CUSMA rules of origin.** Source: Export Development Canada (~86%, Sept 2025), RBC Economics ("almost 90%"). Confidence: Medium-High. This is the single best big-picture stat for the game.
- **But the shield is not universal.** USMCA-compliant goods are exempt from the general tariff, but NOT from the sector tariffs below. That gap is the heart of the lesson.
- **Steel, aluminum, and copper: 50% US tariff (Section 232), no USMCA exemption.** Assessed on the full value of goods made wholly or almost wholly of the metal; derivative products generally 25%; the three metals do not stack on each other. Restructured April 2026. Source: Sandler Travis, Phillips Lytle. Confidence: Medium-High on the 50% headline.
- **Autos and parts: 25% (Section 232).** USMCA-compliant vehicles pay 25% only on their non-US content; USMCA-qualifying parts are largely exempt. Trucks 25%, buses 10%. Source: USTR 2026 USMCA Autos Report to Congress. Confidence: Medium-High.
- **Softwood lumber: about 34.75% all-in** (roughly 24.75% combined antidumping and countervailing duty after the October 2026 review, down from 35.16%, plus a separate 10% Section 232 lumber tariff). Note this is a decades-old trade fight, separate from the 2025-26 tariff war. Source: Global Affairs Canada, US trade press. Confidence: Medium-High. Caveat: a 34.75% duty does not mean 34.75% higher lumber or house prices.
- **The newest flashpoint, Section 338: a 50% US tariff that took effect August 22, 2026 on about $20 billion of Canadian goods, and this one applies EVEN to USMCA-compliant goods.** Source: CBC, wire reporting. Confidence: Medium (well-reported; exact product lists conflict).
- **Energy (crude oil), potash and fertilizer, and critical minerals are EXEMPT from the newest US tariffs.** Confirmed in the Bank of Canada's October 28, 2026 Monetary Policy Report. Confidence: High. Honest nuance for the game: these inputs are overwhelmingly Canadian but currently not tariffed, so treat them as "integration" facts, not "tariff cost" facts.
- **Canada's retaliatory tariffs are in effect: 15%, 25%, or 50% on roughly $27.6 billion of US goods (effective Sept 8, 2026)**, covering dairy, farm equipment, pulp and paper, appliances, electronics, and US-origin video game consoles (raised to 50%). Both countries have tariffs on each other. Source: CNBC, Al Jazeera. Confidence: Medium-High.
- **Negotiation status as of early November 2026: talks resumed after a Nov 1 Trump-Carney call, but no deal is signed and all tariffs remain.** Source: AP/ABC, CTV. Confidence: High that talks are on and no deal yet. This matches CABC's "an opening exists, a durable deal is the goal" framing.

Design takeaway: the game's tariff gates should feel product-specific. A toy gate, a steel-can gate, and a lumber gate should behave differently, and an energy/potash item should show "crosses the border constantly, currently no tariff," which is itself a surprising, honest beat.

## 3. The household cost headline

Use a range with attribution. Never use the old $3,800 or $4,900 numbers as current, they were early-2025 upper bounds.

- **Roughly $1,000 to $1,800 per household per year, depending on the model and which tariffs stay in effect.** Specifics:
  - Yale Budget Lab: about $1,751/year short-run, about $1,292 after consumers substitute (Jan 2026 edition, 2025 dollars); a later 2026 update trended toward ~$1,100-$1,500. Confidence: Medium (site not directly reachable; revised repeatedly).
  - Tax Foundation: about $1,000 for 2025, revised toward roughly $820 for 2026 after the Feb 2026 Supreme Court ruling. Confidence: High (clearly labeled modeled national average).
  - Peterson Institute: about $1,200/year at current tariff levels. Confidence: Medium-High.
- **Suggested game headline:** "Economists estimate tariffs cost the average US household around $1,000 to $1,800 a year" with a footnote naming the three sources and the year. These are modeled national averages, not any one family's bill, worth saying.
- **Prices do not fall back when tariffs ease.** PIIE's point: 2026 prices still reflect the higher tariffs that prevailed before the Feb 2026 Supreme Court decision. Strong teaching beat. Confidence: Medium.

## 4. Game families (the supply-chain journeys and price hooks)

Each family below has: the journey (for the crush-up tiers), the solid facts, and the tariff/price hook (for the surcharge mechanic). Strength rating tells you how defensible the Canada angle is.

### A. The car under the tree (STRONGEST manufacturing story)
- Journey: ore and metal, to stamped and machined parts, to a finished vehicle, hopping the border along the way.
- Facts: a striker plate crosses 4 times (NBC, High); about 50% US content in Canadian-built cars (USTR, High); USMCA requires 75% North American content (CRS, High); rooted in the 1965 Auto Pact (CRS, High).
- Price hook: 25% auto tariff adds about $1,600 per US-assembled vehicle (Anderson Economic Group, year-end 2025, High); steel and aluminum add roughly $250-$2,500 more (AEG via CNBC, Medium); Yale Budget Lab puts new-car prices up about 12-13% short-run, roughly $6,500 (Nov 2025, High). Display a range, cite the source.

### B. The holiday dinner (STRONG, and the most relatable)
- Journey: feed grain and potash, to the bird and the crops, to the plated dinner, crossing both ways.
- Facts: Canada is the world's #2 cranberry producer; Ontario grew 45% of Canada's turkey in 2025 (StatCan, Medium-High); Canada supplies about 86% of US frozen french-fry imports (Medium-High); two-way farm trade in 2024 was about $41.0B US imports from Canada and $28.4B US exports to Canada, making Canada the #2 US ag market (USDA, High); potash, the fertilizer that grows the food, is about 80%+ Canadian-sourced (USGS/TFI, High on dominance).
- Price hook: tariffs raise US food prices by roughly 2.8% (Yale Budget Lab, Medium). Honest nuance: the most tariff-sensitive produce is mostly a Mexico story, not Canada; the Canada food story is potatoes, canola, grains, and potash. Potash is currently tariff-exempt, so play it as "constant crossings, no tariff today."

### C. Maple and holiday baking (STRONG, and iconically Canadian)
- Journey: sap, to syrup, to the cookies and glaze on the table; plus durum wheat to flour to pasta, and canola to cooking oil.
- Facts: Canada is about 73% of world maple production, Quebec is 90.7% of Canada's output (Agriculture Canada, 2024, High); Canada supplies nearly all US maple imports by value (Medium); 80-90% of Canadian canola oil exports go to the US (Farm Credit Canada/USDA, High); the US is the #4 market for Canadian durum, the pasta grain (USDA, Medium-High).
- Price hook: tie to the general food-price lift (~2.8%) and to packaging costs (see paper, below).

### D. The Christmas tree, wrapping, and lumber (STRONG supplier story)
- Journey: logs, to lumber and pulp, to the tree stand, the furniture, the wrapping paper and boxes under it.
- Facts: Canada dominates US fresh Christmas tree imports (StatCan: ~2.03M trees exported in 2023, 96.5% to the US; High on dominance); Canada is the top US supplier of paper and paperboard (~$6.59B in 2025, Medium), which is your wrapping-paper and cardboard link.
- Price hook: softwood lumber carries about a 34.75% all-in duty (Medium-High), a real cost to US home-building. Note trees themselves are currently USMCA-exempt from tariffs, a nice "this one got through free" beat. Keep the lumber-to-price link honest: high duty does not equal 1:1 price rise.

### E. Holiday lights and energy (STRONG integration, EXEMPT from tariffs)
- Journey: Canadian hydro and oil and gas, to the power and fuel, to the lights on the house.
- Facts: Canada supplies about 60-63% of US crude oil imports (EIA, High) and close to 100% of US natural gas imports (EIA, High); Canada is the only foreign supplier feeding the US electric grid directly, via dozens of transmission lines, with New York and New England importing the most, much of it Hydro-Quebec (EIA/CER, High on structure).
- Price hook: play this family as pure integration. Energy is currently exempt from the new tariffs, so the surprise is "this crosses the border constantly and keeps your holidays lit, with no tariff on it today."

### F. Toys and electronics (WEAKEST Canada link, frame honestly)
- Reality: most toys are made in China (about 66-76% of US toy imports), not Canada. Do not imply otherwise.
- Honest connection: toys, lights, and electronics use aluminum and steel inputs that face the 50% metal tariffs, and the broad tariff picture is why US toy prices rose 4.2% in a single month (September 2026) ahead of the holidays (PIIE, Medium). PIIE even published "Santa's sleigh will be weighed down by tariffs."
- Design note: use this family to make the honest point that tariffs reach holiday goods broadly, including from third countries, rather than forcing a false Canada link.

## 5. Defensible numbers you can display in-game

Grounded figures, each safe to show with a source footnote:
- Canned vegetables: up to about +30 cents on a $2 can, roughly 9-15%, from the 50% steel (tinplate) tariff (Consumer Brands Association via Grocery Dive, Medium).
- Major appliances: +5-10%, the documented outcome during the 2018 steel tariffs (BLS/economists via CBS, Medium-High). Strong because it is a measured historical result, not a forecast.
- New vehicle: about +$1,600 (auto tariff, AEG) up to about +$6,500 short-run (Yale). Show as a range.
- Average household: about $1,000-$1,800 per year (Tax Foundation, Yale, Peterson). Range plus footnote.
- Food prices broadly: about +2.8% (Yale Budget Lab).

Rule: whenever the game shows a surcharge, footnote the source and the year, and prefer a range over false precision.

## 6. Framing guardrails (keep it neutral and accurate)

- Tariff gates are a neutral "customs surcharge" obstacle. Never tie them to a party or a leader. Avoid the Carney/Trump political quotes except as neutral "talks are ongoing" context.
- Attribute to institutions: USTR, CBP, USDA, EIA, CRS, Yale Budget Lab, Tax Foundation, Peterson Institute, Anderson Economic Group, Statistics Canada, Bank of Canada.
- Do not state one tariff rate for Canada. Make "it depends on the product and its USMCA status" a feature.
- Use ranges for all household and price figures, with the year.
- Do not revive the outdated $3,800 or $4,900 numbers as current.
- For the border-crossing claim, lead with the verified 4 and present 7-8 as an estimate.
- Energy and potash: integration facts, not tariff-cost facts, because they are currently exempt.
- The win state is a durable deal and lower, more certain prices for both countries, which matches CABC's own language.
